# Outdoor Cycling

## Details

- **Category:** Cardio
- **Difficulty:** beginner
- **Movement pattern:** cardio
- **Equipment:** Cardio
- **Primary muscles:** Quadriceps
- **Secondary muscles:** Calves, Glutes, Hamstrings
- **Aliases:** bike ride, road cycling, cycle commute

## Overview

Riding outdoors for fitness or transport — low-impact cardio that stacks easily into an ordinary day, with fresh air and daylight attached.

## Instructions

1. Set your saddle height so your knee is almost straight at the bottom of the pedal stroke, with a slight bend. Too low is the most common cause of sore knees.
2. Ride in an easy gear and spin rather than mash: aim for a cadence around 70 to 90 revolutions per minute.
3. Sit tall with relaxed shoulders and elbows slightly bent, hands resting lightly on the bars. Look well ahead, not at the front wheel.
4. Keep the effort conversational for most rides — you should be able to speak in short sentences. Save harder efforts for short hills or deliberate intervals.
5. Finish with five minutes of easy spinning, and stretch your calves and hip flexors afterwards.

## Cues

- Light hands, soft elbows
- Spin the gear, don't grind it
- Look where you want to go
- Easy enough to talk, most of the time

## Common mistakes

- Saddle too low or too far forward, so the knees ache
- Grinding a big gear at a low cadence instead of spinning
- Gripping the bars hard and hunching the shoulders
- Riding hard every time and arriving too tired to repeat it later in the week
- Skipping lights, or riding where you cannot be seen

## Safety

This is the one exercise in the library with traffic in it. Wear a helmet, use lights and bright clothing, ride predictably, and assume you have not been seen. Build distance gradually to protect your knees and your seat, and check with a clinician first if you have heart, blood-pressure or balance concerns. Stop for chest pain, dizziness, or breathlessness that feels out of proportion to the effort.

## Longevity notes

### Why this matters for a long life

Cardiovascular fitness is the strongest single predictor of a long, active life, and cycling is one of the easiest ways to accumulate it: it is low impact, so knees and hips tolerate a lot of it, and the effort scales smoothly from a gentle flat loop to a hard climb.

Riding outdoors also stacks habits — daylight, fresh air and, if you commute, cardio you do not have to find separate time for. Three twenty-minute rides that replace a car trip are worth as much as one long weekend ride, and far easier to keep.

### Rhythm and tempo

Spin at 70 to 90 revolutions per minute and keep most rides conversational: the zone 2 effort where you can talk in short sentences. Aim for 150 minutes a week of moderate cardio alongside two strength sessions, and add hills or intervals only once the steady rides feel easy.

### Keeping it for life

Cycling is a habit that survives decades because it doubles as transport and as time outdoors. Judge a ride by whether you would happily do it again tomorrow, not by your average speed.

> The best cardio is the one that gets you out of the house again next week.

## Progression

- **Loading guidance:** Add time or a hill when the ride finishes at a conversational effort and you could have ridden another 15 minutes.
- **Progress to:** Longer rides, rolling terrain, or a regular commute several days a week
- **Regress to:** A flat, short loop, or the stationary bike for the same duration
