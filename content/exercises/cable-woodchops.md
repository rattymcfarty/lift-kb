# Cable Woodchops

## Details

- **Category:** Core
- **Difficulty:** intermediate
- **Movement pattern:** core
- **Equipment:** Cable
- **Primary muscles:** Abs
- **Secondary muscles:** Front Delts, Upper Back
- **Aliases:** cable wood chop, high to low cable chop

## Overview

A standing rotational pull from high to low — the closest thing in the gym to swinging a bag of shopping into the car boot, trained with a load you can control.

## Instructions

1. Set a single handle on the cable at the highest position. Stand side-on to the machine, feet a little wider than your hips, and take the handle with both hands above the shoulder furthest from the machine.
2. Step into a small split stance and let your arms stay long. Brace your trunk as if for a light punch and keep your chest tall.
3. Drive the movement from your trunk and hips, pulling the handle down and across your body to the opposite hip. Your arms only follow — they should not do the work.
4. Let your back foot pivot onto its toes so your knee and hip turn with you. Keep the lower back long; the rotation happens in the hips and upper back, not by rounding.
5. Return along the same path over two to three seconds, resisting the stack all the way back. That is one rep; do 8 to 10 per side.

## Cues

- Pivot the back foot — let the hips turn, not the spine
- Arms are ropes, the trunk is the engine
- Chest tall and ribs down throughout
- Slow on the way back, always

## Common mistakes

- Yanking with the arms and shoulders while the trunk stays still
- Rounding or twisting the lower back instead of pivoting at the hips
- Locking the knees and forgetting to let the back foot turn
- Letting the stack snap you back to the start
- Standing too far from the machine, so the pull comes off balance

## Safety

Rotational work is only as safe as your control: start light enough that you can stop the handle anywhere in the path. If you have an acute lower-back injury, or a disc or facet problem that flares with twisting, regress to a Pallof press — it trains the same muscles with no trunk rotation at all.

## Longevity notes

### Why this matters for a long life

Life is full of rotation: reaching into the back seat, swinging a suitcase into a boot, twisting to pass something across a table. The obliques and the deep core are what make those movements safe, and they are exactly what a diet of straight-line lifting leaves untrained.

The woodchop also rehearses the thing that protects your back — moving a load with a braced trunk and hips that turn, instead of a spine that twists under load. Trained at a moderate weight and a controlled tempo, it teaches your body the difference.

### Rhythm and tempo

Pull down and across over about one second, then take two to three seconds to let the handle travel back. Two or three sets of 8 to 10 per side, two or three times a week, is plenty. Stop the set when the motion stops feeling smooth, not when the muscle burns.

### Keeping it for life

It is one of the few core movements that feels like something you actually do in the world, which makes it easy to keep. Add a little weight only when the tempo stays slow and the hips stay square.

> Train the twist now and you keep the freedom to twist later.

## Progression

- **Loading guidance:** Increase the weight only when 3 sets of 10 per side stay smooth and slow with the hips square at the finish.
- **Progress to:** Low-to-high woodchop, or a heavier stack with a step through the movement
- **Regress to:** Half-kneeling cable chop, or a Pallof press hold
