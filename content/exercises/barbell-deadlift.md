# Barbell Deadlift

## Details

- **Category:** Hinge
- **Difficulty:** intermediate
- **Movement pattern:** hinge
- **Equipment:** Barbell
- **Primary muscles:** Lower Back
- **Secondary muscles:** Calves, Forearms, Glutes, Hamstrings

## Overview

An intermediate barbell hinge-pattern exercise for the lower back. Barbell Deadlift builds posterior-chain strength that protects your back in everyday lifting.

## Instructions

1. Stand with the bar over the middle of your feet, about an inch from your shins. Feet hip-width, toes just slightly out.
2. Hinge at the hips and bend your knees until your hands reach the bar. Take a grip just outside your legs, then set your back long — chest up, shoulders slightly in front of the bar, neck in line with your spine.
3. Pull the slack out of the bar until it is tight against the plates, then take a breath and brace your trunk hard.
4. Push the floor away with your legs, keeping the bar against your legs. Let the hips and shoulders rise together until you are standing tall with your glutes squeezed — do not lean back.
5. Lower the bar the same way you lifted it: hips back first, bar sliding down your legs, taking about two seconds. Set it down under control, reset your brace, and pull again.
6. Work in sets of 3 to 8 reps with two reps in reserve, then stop the set — deadlifts are a lift where the last good rep is the last rep.

## Cues

- Bar over mid-foot, shins close
- Take the slack out, then push the floor away
- Hips and shoulders rise together
- Stand tall, don't lean back
- Hips back first on the way down

## Common mistakes

- Yanking the bar off the floor before the slack is out of it
- Hips shooting up first, so the lift turns into a stiff-legged pull with a rounded back
- Letting the bar drift away from the shins and legs
- Hyperextending and leaning back at the top instead of standing tall
- Bouncing the bar off the floor instead of resetting each rep

## Safety

Deadlifts load the lower back more than any other lift here, so treat the setup as the safety feature: bar over mid-foot, spine long, slack out, then push. Learn it with an empty bar or light dumbbells, keep the last rep as clean as the first, and stop the set when your back rounds. If you have an acute lower-back injury, a history of disc problems, or pain that travels down a leg, get clearance from a clinician first and start with a hip hinge or Romanian deadlift at a light weight.

## Longevity notes

### Why this matters for a long life

Picking things up is not optional. Bags, boxes, grandchildren, the dog — every one of them is a deadlift, and the ability to do it with a straight back is one of the clearest differences between people who stay independent and people who start avoiding the floor.

Trained sub-maximally, the deadlift also builds the grip, glutes and hamstrings that keep you steady on stairs and slopes. The strength you gain here is not the point in itself; being able to lift a suitcase at 75 is.

### Rhythm and tempo

Set up, take the slack out, then push the floor away in one smooth second. Lower under control over about two seconds rather than dropping the bar. Work in sets of 3 to 8 reps with two reps in reserve, once or twice a week — a little goes a long way, and deadlifts take longer to recover from than they feel at the time.

### Keeping it for life

This is a movement to keep grooving, not to max out. Alternate heavier weeks with lighter Romanian deadlifts or kettlebell hinges, and judge progress by how your back feels picking things up in ordinary life.

> The goal is not a heavier bar. It is never having to ask someone else to lift it.

## Progression

- **Loading guidance:** Add weight only when every rep starts with the same flat back and the bar stays against your legs. In the 5 to 8 rep range, leave two reps in reserve.
- **Progress to:** Heavier sets of 5 with the same form, or a trap bar for more load with less spinal demand
- **Regress to:** Romanian deadlift or a hip hinge with a light dumbbell, then kettlebell deadlift from a raised position
