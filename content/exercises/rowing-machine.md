# Rowing Machine

## Details

- **Category:** Cardio
- **Difficulty:** beginner
- **Movement pattern:** cardio
- **Equipment:** Cardio
- **Primary muscles:** Upper Back
- **Secondary muscles:** Abs, Biceps, Glutes, Hamstrings, Lats, Lower Back, Quadriceps
- **Aliases:** erg, indoor rower

## Overview

A full-body, joint-friendly cardio machine where technique does the work: legs first, then trunk, then arms — and the reverse on the way back.

## Instructions

1. Sit with your feet strapped in, shins vertical at the start, and take the handle with a relaxed overhand grip. Sit tall — not slumped back.
2. **The drive:** push the floor away with your legs first, keeping your arms straight and your back at a steady angle. Your legs do roughly half the work.
3. **The finish:** once your legs are almost straight, swing your torso back to about 11 o'clock, then pull the handle to the bottom of your ribs. Elbows pass close to your sides.
4. **The recovery:** reverse it in the same order — arms away, then swing your body forward, then let your knees bend. Take about twice as long coming back as you did driving.
5. Row at a steady 20 to 24 strokes per minute for 10 to 30 minutes, or use the same rhythm for intervals. Stop when your form breaks down, not when you are exhausted.

## Cues

- Legs, then back, then arms — and arms, then back, then legs
- Long stroke, long recovery: drive in one second, return in two
- Push the floor away, don't yank the handle
- Chest proud at the finish, flat back at the catch

## Common mistakes

- Pulling with the arms first instead of driving with the legs
- Rounding the lower back at the catch
- Rushing the recovery so the stroke becomes a tug of war
- Setting the damper to 10 and rowing short, choppy strokes
- Gripping the handle hard enough to tire the forearms first

## Safety

Rowing loads the lower back, so keep it long and braced from the catch to the finish, and shorten your stroke rather than rounding if you tire. Build duration gradually — blisters and a sore lower back in the first week usually mean too much, too soon. Stop and seek advice for chest pain, dizziness, or breathlessness out of proportion to the effort.

## Longevity notes

### Why this matters for a long life

Cardiovascular fitness is the strongest single predictor of a long, active life, and rowing delivers it while training most of your body at once. Because you are seated and the load is smooth, it is friendly to knees, hips and ankles — useful if impact-based cardio like running is uncomfortable.

It also keeps the pulling pattern strong: the same back, arms and trunk that lift a suitcase, close a heavy door or pull yourself out of a chair.

### Rhythm and tempo

The rhythm is the whole skill. Drive for about one second, recover for about two, and hold a stroke rate of 20 to 24 per minute. Aim for the effort where you can still talk in short sentences — the classic zone 2 pace — and build to 150 minutes a week of moderate cardio alongside your strength work.

### Keeping it for life

It is a low-impact, whole-body option that fits in a spare corner, which makes it one of the easiest cardio habits to keep for decades. Track your pace at a fixed stroke rate rather than chasing a personal best every session; small improvements over years are the point.

> The person who still rows easily at 75 started by keeping the easy rows easy.

## Progression

- **Loading guidance:** Add five minutes to a steady row, or one interval, when the last session finished at a conversational effort with your form intact.
- **Progress to:** Longer steady rows, then 4 to 6 rounds of 2 minutes hard with 2 minutes easy
- **Regress to:** Shorter intervals at a lower stroke rate, or a damper setting of 3 to 4
