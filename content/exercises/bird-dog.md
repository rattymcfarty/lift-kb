# Bird Dog

## Details

- **Category:** Core
- **Difficulty:** beginner
- **Movement pattern:** core
- **Equipment:** Bodyweight
- **Primary muscles:** Abs
- **Secondary muscles:** Glutes, Lower Back, Upper Back
- **Aliases:** quadruped opposite arm and leg raise, bird dog exercise

## Overview

A slow, opposite-arm-and-leg floor drill that trains the core to resist rotation and the hips to stay level — the balance and trunk control that keep you steady for life.

## Instructions

1. Start on all fours with hands under shoulders and knees under hips. Look at the floor a little ahead of your hands so your neck stays long.
2. Brace your trunk gently, then reach one arm forward and the opposite leg back until both are in line with your torso.
3. Hold the reach for two seconds. Keep your hips and shoulders level and your ribs down — nothing should move except the arm and leg.
4. Return the hand and knee to the floor over two to three seconds without letting either drop or your back sag.
5. That is one rep. Alternate sides for 8 to 10 slow reps per side.

## Cues

- Reach long, not high
- Hips level, as if a glass of water were balanced on your lower back
- Exhale as you reach, inhale as you return
- Slow counts — two out, two hold, three back

## Common mistakes

- Rotating the hips or shoulders to get more reach
- Lifting the leg too high and arching the lower back instead of lengthening the spine
- Rushing the reps so the arm and leg swing rather than reach
- Shrugging the working shoulder up towards the ear
- Holding the breath for the whole rep

## Safety

Pad your knees and wrists if the floor is hard. If your lower back pinches, shorten the reach until it disappears — a smaller range done well beats a big range with a sagging back. Skip it while an acute back or shoulder injury is settling, and check with a clinician before returning.

## Longevity notes

### Why this matters for a long life

Balance and trunk control are what keep you upright and independent. Falls are one of the leading causes of lost independence after 60, and the muscles that stop a stumble are exactly the ones the bird dog trains: the deep core that resists rotation and the hip and shoulder stabilisers that keep you steady while one limb is busy.

It also teaches the two positions your back needs most — a neutral spine under load, and the ability to move an arm or leg without the spine twisting to follow it. That is the same skill as reaching for a suitcase on a top shelf or catching yourself on a kerb.

### Rhythm and tempo

Slow is the point. Reach for two seconds, hold for two, return over two to three. Two or three sets of 8 to 10 per side, two or three times a week, is plenty — this is a skill, not a burn. Finish the set when the position starts to wobble, not when the muscles fatigue.

### Keeping it for life

It needs no equipment, takes three minutes, and doubles as a warm-up before anything heavier. When it gets easy, slow it down before you add load: a five-second hold at full reach is harder than most people expect.

> You are not training to lift more this month. You are training to get off the floor by yourself at 85.

## Progression

- **Loading guidance:** When 3 sets of 10 per side stay perfectly still, add a five-second hold at full reach, then slow the return to four seconds, then add a light ankle or wrist weight.
- **Progress to:** Bird dog with a five-second hold, or from a bench to extend the range of the arm
- **Regress to:** Dead bug, or opposite arm and leg taps with the hand and foot kept on the floor
